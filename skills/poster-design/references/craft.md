# Craft

## Hierarchy: one thing first
Decide the order of reading before anything else, then make the design enforce it with **size, weight, position and contrast**, in that order of strength.

- **One focal point.** Two things of similar weight cancel each other. The squint render must show a single dominant mass.
- **Scale by jumps.** 1 : 2 : 5 : 14 reads as four levels. 14 / 16 / 18 / 24 reads as a document. If two sizes are within 25% of each other, merge them.
- **Three seconds, three levels.** Stop (headline or image). Stay (the line that explains). Act (date, place, price, link).
- **Whitespace is a shape.** Empty space is what makes the focal point visible. Do not fill it because it is empty.

## Grid
Set the grid before placing anything. 12 columns divides into halves, thirds and quarters; 6 is enough for a simple sheet.

- Margin 5 to 7% of the short side. Text never crosses it; images and display type may bleed.
- Every text block starts on a column edge. If two edges are *nearly* aligned, make them aligned or clearly different.
- Break the grid once, on purpose, with the focal element. A grid with nothing breaking it is a table; a poster with everything breaking it is noise.
- Useful structures: a strong left axis with flush-left text; a top band and a bottom band with the image between; a diagonal; one element cropped by the sheet edge.
- Asymmetry holds attention longer than a centred stack. Centre only when the subject is itself symmetrical or ceremonial.

## Type
Type is the image on most good posters.

- **Two typefaces at most.** One to shout, one to explain. Often one family in two weights is better than two families.
- **Display type:** tight leading (0.82 to 0.95), slightly negative tracking, set large enough to be cropped if it helps. Check every line break: break for sense, never leave one short word alone.
- **Reading type:** leading 1.3 to 1.45, lines of 35 to 55 characters, flush left. Never justified, never centred beyond three lines.
- **Capitals:** small, tracked +8 to +14%, for labels only. A paragraph in capitals is unreadable.
- **Pairing:** contrast in structure, not in mood. A heavy grotesque with a light text face; a sans with one serif italic phrase. Two similar sans faces is a mistake that looks like an accident.
- **Emphasis once.** If one word carries the idea, change one property of it (italic, or colour, or weight). Not all three.
- Proper punctuation: real quotes, a real multiplication sign, a middle dot for separators, non-breaking spaces between a number and its unit.

Faces that hold up at poster size (all open licence): Archivo, Anton, Bricolage Grotesque, Space Grotesk, Figtree, DM Sans, Instrument Serif, Newsreader, Fraunces, IBM Plex Mono for data.

## Colour
- **Ground, ink, one accent.** Give each a job and write the job down. Add a second hue only if it has a different job.
- Take the palette from the subject: the product, the place, the brand. A palette chosen because it is fashionable belongs to nobody.
- Flat colour is the default. A gradient must be explained by light (a lit ground) or it comes out.
- Check the greyscale render: if two areas merge in grey, they compete in colour too.
- Neutrals are not free of character: a warm white and a charcoal read as considered; pure white and pure black read as unset defaults.

## Image
- **Draw it, or use what is supplied.** A drawn object in SVG, a real photograph from the person, or type as image. Never a generated stock-looking picture of people.
- Crop harder than feels safe. A cropped subject is a composition; a small centred subject is a catalogue entry.
- Let the image and the type touch: overlap, tuck behind, replace a letter. Separate zones for picture and text is how templates look.
- One image. If there are several, one is large and the rest are a strip.

## Lineages to draw on
Name the one you are working in. Each is a set of decisions that already work together.

| Lineage | The move | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| International Typographic (Swiss) | Grid, flush-left grotesque, type as image, two or three flat colours | Talks, exhibitions, anything informational |
| Constructivist / Bauhaus | Diagonals, primary colours, geometric blocks, type on an angle | Launches, rallies, energy |
| Editorial / magazine | Big photograph, serif headline, small precise captions | Culture, people, features |
| Product hero | One object, one light, one word; palette from the product | Advertising a thing |
| Brutalist / raw | System fonts, hard edges, black and one colour, visible structure | Music, tech, counter-culture |
| Risograph / print texture | Two inks, overprint, grain, slight misregistration | Community events, zines |
| Corporate humanist | Warm neutral ground, one brand colour, one soft shape, plain speech | Internal communication, healthcare, service |

## What good posters have in common
From looking at award-level work beside template work:

1. The biggest thing is far bigger than the next thing. Often it leaves the sheet.
2. There are two or three colours, and they are flat.
3. Small text is genuinely small, set in a tight block on the grid, with air around it.
4. Something overlaps something else.
5. There is one idea, and you can say it in a sentence.

Template work has the opposite: five things the same size, a badge in every corner, a row of icons, a strip of contact details, glow, and no idea.
